<BlogContent>
I used to play Valorant — an immensely popular first-person shooter game with a dedicated fanbase. But I recently made the decision to quit, and it wasn’t just a spur-of-the-moment thing. I stepped away because I realized something profound: I wasn’t getting any real value from it. Instead of offering enjoyment or satisfaction, the game started to bring me nothing but tension, frustration, and a sense of time slipping away.

When I am truly invested in something, I approach it with passion and obsession. But if a hobby or activity fails to give me that spark, it feels like I’m wasting my time. This was my relationship with Valorant. As much as people say it’s a “social” game that helps with socializing, I don’t agree with that sentiment. Sure, friends can play together, chat, and share experiences virtually, but in my view, real socializing happens face-to-face. Whether it’s meeting friends for coffee or having a conversation on a video call, the quality of human connection is far deeper outside of a virtual world.

More importantly, I noticed some disturbing patterns in the gaming culture surrounding Valorant. When people play, especially when they’re in the heat of a match, they can become completely different versions of themselves. I’ve seen people get angry and abusive when they lose, even toward the friends they’re playing with. The aggression is palpable, and suddenly, their relationships, once built on mutual respect, take a backseat to raw frustration.

It’s as if the virtual game world seeps into their real life — poisoning their behavior, thought process, and mindset. And it’s not just the anger when losing; the reactions to winning can be just as disturbing. I’ve seen people gloat and celebrate with excessive trash talk, as if they’ve just conquered a real battlefield. It felt inhuman, watching friends transform into versions of themselves that I never wanted to be around.

On rare occasions, I encountered a few individuals who played Valorant and managed to stay calm, no matter the outcome. But these people were the exception, not the rule. For the majority, a bad match would ruin their mood for hours, even derailing their productivity. I saw friends missing deadlines, postponing tasks, and sinking into negative emotions — all because of a game. That’s when I knew this environment wasn’t for me.

I crave something more productive, something intellectually stimulating. I want to learn, grow, and challenge myself in ways that games like Valorant simply don’t offer. The gameplay felt repetitive: plant or diffuse a spike, fight enemies, shoot them down — it’s predictable. While some might thrive in that loop, I was growing tired of it. Instead, I found solace in playing chess or engaging in physical sports like badminton, football, or even a brisk walk. These activities nourish both the body and mind, offering genuine satisfaction.

Another factor in my decision to quit was a growing desire to switch to Linux. I’ve always been intrigued by open-source software and the control it offers over my computing experience. Sure, there are games available on Linux too, but I knew that once I made the switch, my focus would shift toward learning and personal development, rather than gaming. Valorant, with its addictive qualities, just didn’t fit into that picture anymore.

In the end, I don’t understand how some people make gaming their hobby, especially games like Valorant, which feel so all-consuming. For me, it was a quick decision to quit: I left Windows, switched to Linux, and moved on from the world of video games. Life is too short to get caught in cycles that don’t serve us, and I’m glad I broke free.
